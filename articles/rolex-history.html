Rolex: The History of a Watchmaking Icon

From Hans Wilsdorf's London business in 1905 to the Oyster and Perpetual innovations, Rolex built its identity around precision, reliability and engineering.

1905 — Hans Wilsdorf's starting point

Rolex traces its history to 1905, when 24-year-old Hans Wilsdorf founded a company in London specializing in the distribution of timepieces. He believed that the relatively new wristwatch could become both elegant and dependable, provided that its movement achieved a high level of precision. Rolex's official history identifies Wilsdorf as the central figure behind the company's early development.

1908 — The name Rolex

In 1908, Wilsdorf adopted the name Rolex. The short name was intended to work well internationally and to look balanced on a watch dial. The name soon became attached to Wilsdorf's broader ambition: making the wristwatch a serious precision instrument.

1910–1914 — Precision becomes the priority

Rolex concentrated heavily on movement quality. In 1910, a Rolex wristwatch became the first wristwatch to receive the Swiss Certificate of Chronometric Precision from the Official Watch Rating Centre in Bienne. In 1914, Kew Observatory in Britain awarded a Rolex wristwatch a Class A precision certificate, a distinction previously associated with marine chronometers.

1919–1920 — Geneva

Rolex moved its base from London to Geneva in 1919, and Montres Rolex S.A. was registered there in 1920. Geneva's established watchmaking ecosystem became an important part of the company's long-term development.

1926 — The Oyster

One of Rolex's defining innovations arrived in 1926 with the Oyster. Rolex describes it as its first completely waterproof and dustproof wristwatch, using a hermetically sealed case to protect the movement. The name “Oyster” became associated with the case and later with a major family of Rolex watches.

1927 — Putting the claim to the test

In 1927, British swimmer Mercedes Gleitze wore a Rolex Oyster during her English Channel crossing. Rolex used the event as a public demonstration of the watch's water resistance. The episode helped establish a pattern that would continue through the company's history: testing watches in demanding real-world conditions.

1931 — The Perpetual rotor

Rolex introduced its Perpetual rotor self-winding mechanism in 1931. The free rotor used the wearer's wrist movement to wind the watch, reducing the need for manual winding. Rolex identifies the Oyster and Perpetual developments as two of the foundational innovations behind its modern watchmaking identity.

1945 and after — Building a long-term institution

Hans Wilsdorf established the Hans Wilsdorf Foundation in Geneva in 1945. After his death in 1960, the foundation became the owner of Rolex. This ownership structure has allowed the company to remain independent while continuing its long-term approach to watchmaking.

Rolex today

Rolex describes itself as an integrated and independent Swiss watch manufacturer headquartered in Geneva. Its modern identity combines mechanical watchmaking with extensive in-house development and manufacturing. The company continues to build on the principles associated with Wilsdorf: precision, reliability, water resistance, self-winding technology and continuous engineering development.

Why Rolex's history matters

The history of Rolex is not simply a story about a luxury brand. It is also a case study in how a company can turn a technical problem into a long-term identity. Precision, protection from water and dust, and automatic winding were engineering challenges before they became recognizable parts of a product family. Rolex's history shows how technical innovation, testing and branding can reinforce one another over decades.
