How to Work at NASA

NASA is often imagined as a place filled with astronauts and rocket scientists. The reality is much broader: engineers, planetary scientists, IT specialists, technicians, project managers, communicators and many other professionals work together on missions that can take years to design, test and operate.

What this article is based on: NASA's current careers information, NASA's “Who Works At NASA?” employee interviews, and NASA's published descriptions of life, values and hiring. Employee experiences vary by center, role and employment status.

Who actually works at NASA?

NASA says its workforce includes more than 18,000 professionals across laboratories, wind tunnels, airfields, control rooms and offices. Its careers site lists more than 150 occupations, not just science and engineering.

ENGINEERING

Engineers

Design, test and operate spacecraft, aircraft, instruments, software and systems. Engineering work can involve everything from thermal protection to propulsion and flight systems.

SCIENCE

Scientists & researchers

Study planets, stars, Earth systems, atmospheres, heliophysics and other scientific questions. Some NASA scientists also work directly with mission instruments and data.

TECHNOLOGY

IT, data & cyber

Modern missions depend on software, data systems, computing infrastructure and cybersecurity as much as they depend on hardware.

OPERATIONS

Technicians & operations

Build, maintain, test and operate hardware and facilities. NASA's own employee stories show that hands-on technical careers are part of the agency's work.

MISSION

Program & project managers

Coordinate schedules, budgets, technical teams, risks and decisions across complex projects where many organizations have to work together.

COMMUNICATION

Writers & communicators

NASA also employs people who explain research and missions to the public through journalism, multimedia, education, photography and other forms of communication.

What do NASA employees say?

In a 2019 NASA Goddard feature, employees Paula Cain, Joy Ng and Geronimo Villanueva described three very different paths into NASA. Cain worked as a thermal blanket technician, Ng as a multimedia producer, and Villanueva as a planetary scientist.

NASA's own employee interviews emphasize that there is no single “NASA type.” Different skills and backgrounds can contribute to the same mission.

Based on NASA Goddard Space Flight Center's “Who Works At NASA? What It Means To Be a NASA Employee” (2019).

NASA's current description of its workplace similarly emphasizes curiosity, teamwork, excellence, resilience and a willingness to adapt. Those are organizational values, not a guarantee that every employee experiences every workplace in exactly the same way.

How do you get hired?

For U.S. civil-service positions, NASA directs applicants to USAJOBS. The process normally involves finding an announcement, preparing a targeted federal resume, applying before the deadline, and—if selected for further consideration—completing interviews and any required assessments.

01

Build the right skills

Choose a field that matches the kind of work you want to do: engineering, physics, computer science, biology, Earth science, communications, business or another relevant discipline.

02

Gain experience

Projects, research, internships, laboratory work, software projects and hands-on technical experience can help demonstrate that you can apply what you study.

03

Use NASA's entry routes

NASA offers internships, including the Pathways program, as well as permanent and temporary federal positions. Contractors and partners also employ people who support NASA missions.

04

Apply carefully

NASA says applicants should match their resume and qualifications to the specialized experience in the specific announcement. Federal applications have requirements that differ from ordinary private-sector applications.

A crucial point for international students

For NASA civil-service employment, U.S. citizenship is generally required, with extremely rare exceptions. That does not mean space careers are limited to U.S. citizens: NASA works with universities, international partners, contractors and other organizations, and the wider space sector includes agencies and companies around the world.

For someone outside the United States, the practical lesson is to separate two goals: working directly for NASA and building a career in space science or technology. The second path has many more international possibilities.

What is the work really like?

It is less like a science-fiction movie and more like a long, highly collaborative engineering and research process. A mission can involve years of design reviews, simulations, testing, documentation, risk analysis, meetings and repeated problem-solving before the public sees a launch or a scientific result.

That is also why NASA's emphasis on safety, integrity, teamwork and excellence matters. A small technical decision can affect hardware, schedules, budgets and sometimes an entire mission.
