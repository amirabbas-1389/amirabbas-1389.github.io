Watergate: The Scandal That Changed the American Presidency

The Watergate affair began with a break-in at Democratic National Committee offices in 1972 and developed into a major constitutional and political crisis that ended with Richard Nixon's resignation.

How Watergate began

In the early hours of June 17, 1972, five burglars were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters at the Watergate complex in Washington, D.C. The National Archives records that the burglars were linked to President Richard Nixon's reelection organization. The break-in involved attempts to obtain information and install surveillance equipment. citeturn0search0turn0search2

From burglary to a wider investigation

The arrests initially looked like a campaign-related crime, but investigations uncovered a broader pattern of political misconduct and efforts to conceal what had happened. Congressional investigators, prosecutors, journalists and the courts increasingly focused on the White House's role in the affair. The Senate Watergate investigation and testimony from former Nixon aides brought the issue into national view. citeturn0search0turn0search3

The White House tapes

One of the most important discoveries was that Nixon had a system for recording conversations in the White House. Prosecutors and Congress sought access to the recordings. Nixon claimed executive privilege, but the dispute ultimately reached the Supreme Court.

On July 24, 1974, the Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Nixon had to comply with a subpoena for the tapes. The recordings became central evidence in the investigation. citeturn0search3

The constitutional crisis

As the investigation progressed, the House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment accusing Nixon of obstruction of justice, abuse of power and contempt of Congress. Before the full House could vote, Nixon announced that he would resign. citeturn0search0turn0search3

Nixon resigns and Gerald Ford becomes president

Richard Nixon's resignation took effect on August 9, 1974, making him the first U.S. president to resign from office. Vice President Gerald R. Ford was sworn in as the 38th president that same day. The National Archives documents the transition with photographs of both Nixon's departure and Ford's swearing-in. citeturn0search1

Ford's pardon of Nixon

On September 8, 1974, President Ford granted Nixon a full, free and absolute pardon for federal offenses Nixon might have committed or taken part in while president. Ford later explained that he believed the country needed to move beyond the Watergate crisis. The pardon remained controversial and became an important part of Ford's presidency. citeturn0search4

Why Watergate matters

Watergate demonstrated how investigations, journalism, Congress, the courts and presidential institutions can interact during a constitutional crisis. It also produced lasting debates about executive power, accountability, political ethics and the limits of presidential authority. The National Archives describes the affair as a crisis that tested and ultimately affirmed the rule of law. citeturn0search0
